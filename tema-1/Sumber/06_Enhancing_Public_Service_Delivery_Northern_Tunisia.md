ABSTRACT

This study investigates how citizens in Northern Tunisia engage with public services
through a multichannel management (MCM) framework, integrating both digital and
traditional channels. Drawing on survey data from the Beja governorate, it examines
how demographic factors, task complexity, and digital access influence citizens’ channel
choices. Findings reveal that while digital tools are widely available, offline channels
remain dominant due to trust, usability concerns, and cultural preferences. Online
platforms are primarily used for information-seeking, whereas face-to-face interactions
are preferred for complex or high-stakes tasks. The study challenges assumptions of
automatic digital migration, highlighting the need for context-sensitive, inclusive service
strategies. It contributes to the literature by situating channel behavior within a North
African context, offering empirical insights that expand multichannel governance
theories beyond the Global North.

Introduction
The demand for efficient and accessible public services has intensified globally, particularly in regions
facing socioeconomic challenges and digital transformation pressures (Margetts & Dunleavy, 2013; West,
2004). Northern Tunisia presents a compelling case for examining public service delivery mechanisms,
characterized by its unique demographic composition and evolving digital landscape. While extensive
research has documented citizens’ channel selection behaviors and multichannel management (MCM)
strategies in developed contexts (Chatfield & Reddick, 2018; Pieterson & Ebbers, 2020), significant gaps
remain in understanding these dynamics in North African settings, where cultural norms and infrastructure constraints may yield distinct patterns of channel use.
Recent research has substantially advanced the understanding of channel choice behavior. Numerous
studies since 2010 have systematically examined how citizens navigate between digital and traditional
service channels based on task complexity, trust factors, and demographic characteristics (Madsen &
Kræmmergaard, 2015; Wirtz, 2022). For instance, Pieterson and Ebbers (2020) demonstrated that while
digital channels dominate information retrieval, citizens consistently revert to traditional channels for
complex transactions, a phenomenon they term ‘channel switching behavior’. Similarly, Kernaghan (2015)
and Chatefield & Reddick (2018) have established robust frameworks for analyzing channel preferences
across different demographic groups (Chatfield & Reddick, 2018; Kernaghan, 2015).
In this study, Multichannel Management (MCM) refers to the strategic integration of diverse service
delivery channels including digital platforms, face-to-face services, printed media, and community-based
mechanisms to enhance accessibility and citizen satisfaction (Santamaría-Philco & Wimmer, 2018; Wirtz
et al., 2023). Channel Choice (CC) is understood as the process by which citizens select a particular service channel based on factors such as task complexity, trust, digital literacy, and accessibility (Pieterson
et al., 2017). These definitions guide the analytical framework of this research.

Contemporary research now recognizes MCM as a mature and strategically adaptive approach, evolving from earlier ambiguities into a widely accepted model for service optimization (Alexopoulos et al.,
2019; Wirtz & Birkmeyer, 2015). Rather than debating basic definitions, recent scholarship focuses on
channel synergy and integration tailored to local needs (Castilla et al., 2021). This study builds upon this
consensus while emphasizing the need for localized, context-sensitive applications in regions such as
Northern Tunisia.
In the Tunisian context, where digital infrastructure coexists with strong traditions of face-to-face governance, examining channel preferences offers valuable insights. While global patterns of channel use are
well-documented (Madsen & Kræmmergaard, 2016), Tunisia’s particular combination of high mobile penetration (92% smartphone ownership) and persistent offline service preferences presents an important
opportunity to test and refine existing MCM theories. This study builds on established channel choice
literature while addressing the critical gap in North African empirical research, aiming to contribute both
to academic understanding and practical improvements in public service delivery.
Northern Tunisia presents a distinctive case due to its sociocultural and infrastructural landscape,
where service delivery is shaped by uneven internet connectivity, strong traditions of relational governance, and varying levels of digital literacy. Unlike Northern Europe or North America, where multichannel management has matured within high-trust, digitally integrated societies (Abdelsalam et al., 2013;
Verhoef et al., 2005), Tunisia’s service ecology reflects a hybrid system in transition. The reliance on offline
channels among even technologically capable individuals points to a complex interplay of access, trust,
and usability, reinforcing calls for context-sensitive multichannel strategies (Criado & Villodre, 2018;
Klievink et al., 2017). Our findings extend existing models by situating channel behavior within a North
African context that resists universalist assumptions.
Despite extensive literature on multichannel behavior in digitally mature societies, there remains a
critical lack of empirical research in North African contexts, where digital adoption is uneven and trust
in institutions varies. This study addresses that gap by examining the interplay between channel choice,
user demographics, and task complexity in Northern Tunisia.
The primary objective of this study is to evaluate how citizens in a semi-digitized public service environment choose between online and offline channels and to identify the factors influencing these
decisions.
By analyzing channel preferences across demographic groups and types of public service needs, this
article contributes to the literature by offering a localized perspective that challenges universal models
of digital service adoption and emphasizes the importance of cultural and infrastructural factors in multichannel management.
The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows: First, we review the literature on multichannel management and channel choice behavior, with particular attention to recent theoretical advances. Next, we
present our methodology for data collection in Northern Tunisia, followed by an analysis of channel
preferences across different demographic groups and service types. Finally, we discuss implications for
both theory and practice, highlighting how our findings compare with and extend existing research in
the field.

Literature review
The study of multichannel management (MCM) and channel choice (CC) in public service delivery has
undergone significant transformation over the past three decades. What began as an optimistic belief in
the dominance of digital channels has evolved into a nuanced understanding of how multiple service
channels interact, influenced by technological, organizational, and user-related factors. This literature
review traces the development of MCM and CC research, highlighting key theoretical contributions,
empirical findings, and emerging trends while identifying gaps for future inquiry.

Early optimism and the emergence of multi-channeling (1990s–Early 2000s)
The advent of the Internet in the 1990s sparked widespread anticipation that digital channels would
revolutionize service delivery across both public and private sectors. Scholars and practitioners

predicted that online platforms would replace traditional face-to-face and physical channels due to their
advantages in accessibility, speed, and cost-efficiency (Castilla et al., 2021). In the private sector, businesses rapidly adopted e-commerce models, expecting digital channels to become the primary means
of distribution (Bélanger & Carter, 2008; Dijk & Hacker, 2018). Similarly, in the public sector, early
e-government initiatives promoted online services as a way to enhance efficiency and citizen engagement (Moon, 2013).
However, by the early 2000s, it became clear that digital transformation alone could not address the
diverse needs of all users. Research revealed that while online channels were effective for simple transactions, many citizens and customers still preferred traditional methods for complex or sensitive interactions (Riggins & Mitra, 2007). This realization led to the emergence of multi-channeling, where
organizations provided services through multiple parallel channels online, phone, and in-person rather
than relying on a single dominant medium (Vries, 2020).
During this period, scholars began examining the factors influencing channel preferences. Lee (2002)
found that demographic characteristics such as age, education, and gender significantly shaped individuals’ choices, reinforcing the need for diverse service delivery options (Lee, 2002). Meanwhile, Verhoef
et al. (2005) developed foundational theories comparing online and offline channels, demonstrating that
each had distinct strengths: digital platforms excelled in convenience, while physical interactions provided trust and personalization (Verhoef et al., 2005). These insights laid the groundwork for more
sophisticated MCM frameworks in subsequent years.

Theoretical advancements and sector-specific insight (mid-2000s–2010s)
As digital adoption grew, research on MCM and CC became more refined, moving beyond descriptive
studies to develop structured models of channel selection and integration. A pivotal contribution came
from Pieterson (2010), who emphasized that multi-channel strategies should not merely offer parallel
options but strategically align channels with specific service requirements (W. Pieterson, 2010). This perspective was further developed by Wirtz and Langer (2017), who introduced a comprehensive MCM
framework identifying four key dimensions: channel characteristics, user segmentation, organizational
integration, and performance measurement. Their work highlighted the importance of coordination,
rather than mere coexistence of channels, a concept later expanded upon by Santamaria-Philco et al.
(2019) under the label of channel synergy (Santamaria-Philco et al., 2019).
In the public sector, studies by Reddick (2005) and Thomas and Streib (2003) revealed persistent gaps
between digital service availability and actual usage. Citizens frequently turned to government websites
for information but preferred phone or in-person interactions for complex tasks, particularly when trust
or clarity was a concern (Thomas & Streib, 2003). These findings challenged earlier assumptions that
digital channels would naturally supplant traditional ones and underscored the need for flexible,
user-centric approaches. Millard (2007) formalized this idea with the concept of ‘flexi-channeling’, distinguishing between ‘single-channelers’ (those reliant on one method, typically in-person) and
‘flexi-channelers’ (those who dynamically switched between channels based on need) (Millard, 2007).
Meanwhile, private-sector research provided valuable comparative insights. While businesses increasingly prioritized cost-effective digital platforms, sectors like banking retained physical branches for
high-stakes transactions, illustrating that channel optimization required balancing efficiency with user
preferences (Vives, 2019). These lessons informed public-sector strategies, particularly in e-government,
where scholars like Pina et al. (2009) and Yildiz (2007) documented the enduring role of face-to-face
services despite digital advancements (Pina et al., 2009; Yildiz, 2007).

Maturation of MCM research: integration, context, and equity (2010s–present)
The past decade has seen MCM scholarship mature, with a stronger emphasis on empirical validation,
contextual factors, and equity considerations. Pieterson et al. refined their earlier models by incorporating new evidence on channel-switching behaviors, identifying three core determinants of CC: task characteristics (e.g. complexity, urgency), user characteristics (e.g. digital literacy, trust), and channel
characteristics (e.g. accessibility, privacy) (Pieterson & Ebbers, 2020). This tripartite model has been

A significant development has been the recognition of national and cultural influences on channel

channel choice differently depending on institutional trust and digital infrastructure (Abdelsalam et al.,
2013). Similarly, Klievink et al. (2017) highlighted how intermediary organizations (e.g. libraries and community centers) shaped channel use patterns, particularly among digitally excluded groups
(Panagiotopoulos et al., 2019). These findings challenged the notion of a universal channel hierarchy and
underscored the need for context-sensitive MCM strategies (Pieterson et al., 2017).
Parallel advancements in digital divide research further enriched MCM scholarship. Early studies
focused on access gaps (van Dijk et al., 2007), but contemporary work emphasizes usage and outcome
disparities (van Deursen & Helsper, 2015). For instance, Chen and Dimitrova (2006) showed that even

mistrust (Chen & Dimitrova, 2006). Ebbers et al. (2020) addressed this through digital mediation strategies, such as training programs and hybrid service designs, which bridge gaps between online and
offline users (Pieterson & Ebbers, 2020).
Recent systematic reviews have consolidated these insights. Madsen and Kræmmergaard’s (2016) analysis of 127 CC studies established robust typologies of choice determinants, while Santamaria-Philco et al.
(2019) synthesized evidence on channel synergy, arguing that optimal MCM requires active coordination
rather than passive coexistence. Additionally, behavioral insights have gained traction, with Pieterson and
Ebbers (2020) incorporating nudge theory and habit formation into CC models to explain why users
often default to suboptimal channels.
This literature shows that the evolution of MCM and CC research reflects a broader shift from
techno-optimism to evidence-based, user-centric approaches. Early expectations of digital dominance gave
way to recognition of the complex interplay between technology, organizational structures, and human
behavior. Today, the field emphasizes strategic integration, equity, and adaptability, offering valuable
insights for policymakers seeking to optimize public service delivery. However, as digital landscapes continue evolving, future studies must explore AI-driven channel management, longitudinal behavior patterns,
and inclusive design principles to ensure MCM systems serve all citizens effectively. Here, after a summary

Hypothesis
Research indicates that citizens use a variety of channels to communicate with government authorities,
a phenomenon driven by the need for flexibility and accessibility in accessing services. According to
Pieterson and Ebbers (2008), the choice of service delivery channels reflects differences in task complexity, personal preferences, and accessibility (Pieterson & Ebbers, 2008). Citizens often interchange between
digital and offline channels based on the nature of the service required, as observed by Reddick (2005),
who found that complex tasks are more frequently resolved using personal interaction, while information
retrieval is commonly done online (Reddick, 2005).
Additionally, Millard (2007) emphasizes the concept of ‘flexi-channeling’, where citizens demonstrate
adaptability in their channel usage, selecting based on convenience, familiarity, and trust. Lee (2002) also
suggests that age, education, and technological comfort influence channel preference (Lee, 2002; Millard,
2007). These studies prove that citizens actively use multiple channels to engage with government services.
Therefore, to determine whether people use numerous channels (‘multi (or flexi) channelers’), the first
hypothesis is formulated:
H1:	Citizens use many channels to communicate with government authorities.

A deeper examination of the factors influencing citizens’ preference for offline channels over online
ones reveals a multifaceted issue beyond mere access to digital devices. Fundamental to this is the ‘digital divide’, a concept introduced by Cai (2008), which highlights not only the disparity in physical access
to technology but also the proficiency in utilizing these tools effectively (Cai, 2008). Despite the proliferation of connected devices, many citizens lack the necessary digital literacy, inhibiting their ability to
leverage online channels for interacting with government services. Moreover, Chen & Dimitrova (2006)
suggest that demographic characteristics such as age, education, and income levels significantly impact
individuals’ internet usage patterns. (Chen & Dimitrova, 2006).
However, it is essential to note the complex interplay of these factors; while some studies cite gender
as a determinant of online engagement, findings are inconsistent, reflecting the need for nuanced analysis. This complexity is mirrored in Helsper’s (2012) research on digital inclusion, demonstrating how
socioeconomic and behavioral barriers often shape preferences for offline communication methods
(Helsper, 2012). For many, traditional face-to-face interactions inherently offer a sense of trust and reliability that digital interfaces struggle to provide. Li et al. (2019) further argue that trust and perceived
ease of use are crucial in channel selection, with offline channels often seen as safer and more straightforward. This perception is particularly pervasive among populations with limited exposure to digital
interfaces, reaffirming the preferential use of more familiar offline methods (Li et al., 2019).
These insights underscore the necessity for a comprehensive approach to addressing the digital divide.
They suggest that government initiatives should focus on increasing access to technology, enhancing
digital literacy, and fostering trust in online systems. These factors can significantly aid in strategizing
effective multichannel management that resonates with citizens’ diverse needs and preferences.
H3:

Men use online channels more frequently than women.

H4:

Younger citizens use online channels more regularly than older people.

H5:

More educated people are likely to use online channels than less educated people.

Research suggests that the specific characteristics of a task can significantly impact the channels citizens choose when interacting with government services. For instance, Noble et al. (2005) and Verhoef
et al. (2005) illustrate that simple transactions, like information retrieval or simple inquiries, are often
more efficiently handled online due to speed and convenience. Conversely, more complex tasks, such as
obtaining permissions or completing legal transactions, might require the depth of understanding and
personal interaction that offline channels offer (Noble et al., 2005; Verhoef et al., 2005).
Furthermore, Goldsmith and Crawford (2014) argue that the perceived complexity of a task can lead
citizens to seek face-to-face assistance, reflecting a preference for responsiveness and nuanced understanding that digital platforms often lack (Goldsmith & Crawford, 2014).

often rely on personal interactions for assurance in service delivery processes (Li et al., 2019).
These insights highlight governments’ importance in maintaining robust offline service options while

Methodology
This study employs a face-to-face survey methodology to ensure inclusivity across digital literacy levels
and device ownership. This approach is particularly relevant in regions like Northern Tunisia, where digital inequalities persist despite high mobile penetration (Chen & Dimitrova, 2006; Helsper, 2012). Face-toface methods are recognized for improving response rates and data quality in populations with variable
ICT access (Tourangeau et al., 2013). The selection of the Beja governorate, currently piloting multichannel municipal services, offers a strategic case for studying citizen interaction across channel types. The
sample includes both digitally connected and disconnected individuals, allowing the study to capture
channel preferences beyond digitally engaged subgroups—a critical gap identified in multichannel
research (Reddick, Abdelsalam and Elkadi, 2012; van Dijk, 2007). The combination of frequency distribution, OLS regression, and non-parametric tests aligns with best practices in public service behavior
research where data distributions vary and variables include both ordinal and categorical measures (Field,
2013). This mixed-method quantitative design thus ensures analytical robustness while grounding results
in the realities of a hybrid governance system.
The research was conducted in the Beja governorate in northeastern Tunisia (Figure 1). This governorate encompasses eight municipalities, four of which currently offer both online and offline municipal
services as part of a national trial program designed to assess electronic service delivery. This initiative
is scheduled to operate from 2022 to 2026, with plans for expansion to more communities. The face-toface survey gathered 259 genuine responses acceptable for analysis. A total of 123 replies were collected
from the four municipalities that provide both offline and online services.
The current research was intentionally designed to involve people who do not use connected devices,
as they still comprise a sizable proportion of the population. As a result, the questionnaire was administered through face-to-face interviews. Citizens who agreed to participate were asked about their use of
smartphones, laptops, internet data plans, and home internet access.
The sample consisted of 48% female and 52% male participants, with an average age of 39. In 46%
of cases, the respondents had a university education, whereas in 40% of cases, they had a high school
education. The data shows a significant gender gap, with males earning 69% more than females, exceeding the minimum wage of 150 USD. Ninety-two percent of respondents possessed a smartphone with a
data plan, and there was no notable difference in this regard across genders. Approximately 24% of the
participants demonstrated infrequent use of ICT, while the remaining 76% had regular to frequent use.

In the study, respondents were asked to indicate whether they utilized each channel in their interactions with their municipality. The data shows that people interact with government agencies differently. A new variable was introduced to confirm H1 statistically. At first, the questions were nominal
(yes/no). The number of channels used was determined by adding the points assigned to each variable

The study used a frequency distribution test to determine whether the observed values were more

people use multiple channels. People typically employ three channels (3.02) when communicating with

Analysis
In examining how citizens interact with public authorities, data reveals that a significant number of individuals engage through multiple channels, identifying them as multi-channel or flexi-channel users (H1).
This observation aligns with prior academic research, demonstrating that citizens utilize a variety of
channels to fulfill different needs. Notably, there seems to be no strong inclination towards preferring
online services over traditional methods. Consistent with Hypothesis 2 (H2), citizens frequently resort to
offline channels, such as counter services and printed documents, alongside online platforms.
The study examines various demographic and socioeconomic factors to investigate the differences
observed in channel usage. Specifically, the findings under Hypothesis 3 (H3) indicate that gender does
not significantly influence channel preference; both men and women utilize offline and online channels
in similar proportions. However, considerable differences emerge concerning age (H4) and educational
level (H5). The data consistently shows that age plays an essential role; older individuals are more inclined
towards offline channels, whereas younger respondents, including those between 30 and 59, still predominantly rely on offline methods.
Education level and Information and Communication Technology (ICT) competence also emerge as key
determinants. Individuals with higher education levels or advanced ICT skills are more likely to prefer online
channels compared to those with lower levels of education or less technological expertise. Those with a less
educational background or lower ICT skills tend to gravitate significantly more towards offline channels than
individuals with moderate to high levels of education and ICT skills. Despite this, it is worth noting that the
majority of respondents, regardless of age or education, prefer offline channels, with a preference rate of

earlier studies, reinforcing the understanding that citizens employ multiple channels depending on the

Northern Tunisia. Simple tasks, such as information retrieval, are predominantly conducted online, while
complex administrative actions prompt a shift to offline interactions. This aligns with Venkatesh et al.

strong preference for face-to-face interaction, even among younger and educated populations, suggests
that the relational nature of governance in Tunisia deeply influences how needs are mapped onto channels. Thus, task complexity alone cannot predict channel choice without accounting for trust, familiarity,
and service maturity within the local governance structure.
Ultimately, while citizens utilize a range of channels, there is a clear preference for offline interactions, which
are perceived as more favorable (H8). The study also identifies variations between individuals with access to a
connected device and those without. Those without a connected device tend to use fewer channels and hold
a more favorable view of offline channels compared to their online-engaged counterparts. This analysis significantly enhances our understanding of channel preferences in citizen- public authority interactions. It underscores the need for strategies that bridge the digital divide while catering to diverse user preferences.

Conclusion
Summarizing the complex relationships inherent in multichannel management across Northwestern Tunisia’s
municipalities reveals the importance of an integrated approach to communication channels in improving public service engagements. This study emphasizes the importance of rethinking government service delivery
structures, with a focus on integrating both offline and online channels for increased efficiency and inclusivity
across varied demographic groups. Such an integrated approach is consistent with broader trends in public
administration studies, as observed by Alexopoulos et al. (2019), who argue that combining traditional and
digital communication strategies is critical for addressing accessibility gaps.
Santamaria-Philco et al. (2019) explore channel synergy, which advocates for an innovative approach
of public service delivery based on cross-channel collaboration. This model addresses and lowers the
redundancies found in separate channel topologies, resulting in a seamless service environment that
matches with citizens’ various preferences and habits (Santamaria-Philco et al., 2019). Furthermore, findings from the Technology Acceptance Model shed light on how perceived relevance and ease of use
influence channel selection, lending credence to the idea that well-integrated channels can increase user
happiness and engagement.
One important component of this study is the persisting digital gap, which is defined by unequal
access to connected devices and necessitates targeted policy initiatives. According to Chen and Dimitrova
(2006), overcoming such discrepancies requires not just technological infrastructure upgrades, but also
improvements in digital literacy to ensure fair channel access. The fact that persons without personal
digital access can still engage online demonstrates adaptive resilience, which needs inclusive digital strategies that accommodate a wide range of access levels and proficiency.
Furthermore, enhancing multichannel management necessitates a cultural shift toward citizen-centric
service design. This transition is helped by interactive governance models that encourage a participatory
approach and incorporate citizen feedback into service development procedures (Reddick, 2005). By putting citizens at the center of channel strategy design, government bodies can improve service relevance,
build trust, and inspire greater involvement.
In conclusion, this study gives critical insights into the complicated environment of multichannel management in public services, laying foundations for future research. By encouraging debate and constant
modification, this strategy has the potential to change public sector service delivery into a more responsive, adaptable, and egalitarian system. Such systemic reforms are critical for developing resilient civic
institutions that respond to citizens’ changing needs and ambitions, assuring long-term involvement and
improved public service outcomes in an increasingly digital environment.
