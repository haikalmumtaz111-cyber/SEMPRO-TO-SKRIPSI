In the past decades, the topic of how and why citizens choose certain service channels to interact with governments has received widespread attention as it is an important component of government service channel strategies. Most of the existing work, however, suffers from two problems. The first is an ongoing focus on a limited set
of possible determinants, making it hard to assess which of these factors are most important. The second is the
underlying assumption that citizens rationally assess their situation and based on the task at hand choose the
best fitting service channel. In this article we challenge both assumptions. We review the work on channel choice
and rationality in decision making. Based on this review we propose and empirically test a model combining different determinants and decision making processes. Our findings show that citizens sometimes choose channels
rationally and sometimes irrationally. The task at hand, personal characteristics, and situation trigger which factors are most important. As a consequence, models focused on channel strategies should focus less on rational
‘matching’ and more on situational factors.

1. Introduction
When government agencies started deploying electronic service
channels in the 1990s, providing services to citizens and businesses,
the expectation was not only that governments would save money by
delivering services through the cheaper online channels, but also that
citizens would adopt these channels quickly and start utilizing them
(Pieterson & Van Dijk, 2007). However, several studies from various
countries, such as Australia (Australian Government, 2005), Canada
(Erin Research, 2003), The Netherlands (Bongers, Holland, Vermaas, &
Vandeberg, 2004), and Switzerland (Berner Fachhochschule & Unisys,
2005), showed that a decade later governmental agencies still faced
high numbers of contacts via more traditional service channels, such
as phone and front desk. Even now, another decade later, we often see
that clients in citizen-to-government or business-to-government interactions still prefer telephone or front desk over website (e.g. Reddick &
Turner, 2012; Van den Boer, 2014, Reddick & Anthopoulos, 2014,
Kræmmergaard & Østergaard Madsen, 2015).

The obvious question is why this is still the case. If electronic channels are superior to traditional channels, as many governments seem
to believe, then why are citizens still using the traditional channels? Research focusing on citizens' channel choices, as well as models focusing
on the incorporation of these behaviors in multi-channel management
service delivery models often center on the argument that channels
have characteristics that render them suited for different types of
tasks (see e.g. Ebbers, Pieterson, & Noordman, 2008). Hence, different
types of channels should be deployed for different types of services.
Given the complexity or ambiguity of certain tasks, this could indeed explain why citizens still keep using traditional channels for many services
(see Daft & Lengel, 1986 for a discussion of the media richness concept
that connects tasks to media).
However, this thought assumes that citizens are able to assess the
characteristics of the task at hand and subsequently match the characteristic of this task with properties of the available service channels.
This is a very rational view on decision making that expects citizens to
have full information and objectively weigh pros and cons of tasks and
channels.
There are two problems with this assumption. The first is that research has already shown that channel choice is not merely a function
of task characteristics. Studies have found that factors as demographics
and personal characteristics, situational constraints, experiences, the
nature of the interaction, values that citizens place on public service,
the overall satisfaction that citizens place on the service that they

The second problem is that the idea that decision making processes
are inherently rational has been challenged as well. Several studies have
argued that people do not actively weigh task characteristics, but rather
make their choices based on habits (Pieterson & Van Dijk, 2007). Max
Weber (1922) has argued that these are not two competing approaches.
Rather, they are simply different ways of making decisions. Based on the
situation, people use one or the other strategy. The labels Weber uses for
those different strategies are rational and irrational social action.
If we, finally, want to improve the efficiency of governmental service
delivery, we need to start by increasing our understanding of the channel choice process and to that end, we need to move beyond matching
tasks to channels but also incorporate other factors that may lead to
more, or less, rational processes of decision making.
Goal of this contribution is twofold. The first is to summarize the
state of the art in eGovernment research focusing on channel choice.
The second is to propose and empirically test a model of channel choice
that goes beyond studying intentional use, but expands towards actual
use. This model is based on the summary of the existing literature and
theories reflecting both rational and irrational channel choice
determinants.

2. Channel choice
In recent years, various studies have been conducted on how and
why citizens choose and use the different service channels. One of the
most comprehensive one is the study of Pieterson (2009) (who also
provides a comprehensive overview of the channel choice literature
prior to 2009). This study on information needs of taxpayers suggests
that, at least for tax related issues, citizens primarily follow their habits
in channel selection. Pieterson (2009) also suggests that, secondarily,
certain factors (situational, emotional, etc.) can trigger a more rational
channel choice process.
In case of that second instance decision process “situational, and
emotional factors as well as habits do not play a role anymore”
(Pieterson, 2009, p. 235). Once the second process takes place,
Pieterson's (2009) findings suggest that citizens choose channels that
suit their task and its given characteristics best, a so-called taskchannel elaboration. The elaboration process depends on of the nature
the task on the one hand and the characteristics of the channel on the
other hand.
More recently, Reddick and Anthopoulos (2014) studied channel
choice behavior in Canada. Their results show that, even though the
use of traditional channels is declining (traditional office visits declined
from 64% in 2005 to 47% in 2012), the use of the website increased to
47% in 2008, and then declined to 38% in 2012 as well. The authors
(2014) attribute this decline to the website's limited capabilities in
terms of problem solving. Furthermore, their data illustrate the differences in usage between the different channels. The results showed
that office visits were most often used for applications/registrations
(62%); voice phone-calls were preferred for problem solving (68%);
and government websites were most often used for information or advice retrieval (53%). Reddick and Anthopoulos (2014) found four factors
to be key predictors of channel usage: the digital divide; user satisfaction with the channel; nature of the transaction; and finally security
and privacy.
Kræmmergaard and Østergaard Madsen (2015) studied how citizens in Denmark use channels and can be guided towards online services. They found that the Internet is the primary channel to conduct
transactions, while the phone is the main channel to solve problems
arising with these transactions. This seems to suggest that people develop certain preferences to solve certain types of problems. This is in line
with Pieterson (2009), who argued that experiences shape how people

channels.
This overview of studies suggests that a wealth of different factors
may influence channel choice. Sadly virtually no studies exist that incorporate all these variables mentioned. In our empirical work (see below),
we have tried to fill this gap by testing a model of channel choice that
includes task related factors, channel related factors, habit, and personal
characteristics. However, before we present this model we first discuss
the decision making process in more detail.
3. Theoretical framework
Throughout this article, we have touched on a key theoretical issue
pertaining to channel choice behavior and that is the question to
which extent channel choice is a rational process or an irrational process
steered by habits. As discussed above, there are two streams of thought
on decision making. The first is that of the rational actor and rational
choice defined by Hahn and Hollis (1979) as:
“Given the set of available actions, the agent chooses rationally if there
is no other action available to him the consequence of which he prefers
to that of the chosen action (Hahn & Hollis, 1979, p. 4)”.
This implies that the actor will carefully weigh all choices available
and subsequently choose the best possible alternative. This is an assumption that falls perfectly in line with multi-channel service delivery
models, such as the one proposed by Ebbers et al. (2008).
The second choice process, as suggested by Pieterson (2009), is that
of the non-rational habitual decision maker. Habits are “learned sequences of acts that become automatic response to specific situations,
which may be functional in obtaining certain goals or end states”
(Verplanken, Aarts, & van Knippenberg, 1997, p. 540). Various studies
have indeed shown that many decisions are made via habits instead of
via reasoned actions (Aarts, Verplanken, & Knippenberg, 1998; Sutton,
1994; Verplanken et al., 1997).
So how are decisions made? Are people rational beings that carefully
assess situations and elaborate, or does no such process take place and
do people choose channels based on their habits, driven by
experiences?
According to various theorists, both can exist at the same time. The
adaptive decision maker hypothesis is one attempt to integrate different
views on rationality, it assumes that “the use of various decision strategies is an adaptive response of a limited-capacity information processor
to the demands of complex task environments” (Payne, Bettman, &
Johnson, 1993). In other words, depending on the situation, for example
a lack of time or urgency, people will change the level of rationality in
their process. According to Payne et al. (1993), this depends on the desired accuracy of the decision making process and the desire to minimize the (cognitive) effort required to make to make this decision.
A similar, often criticized but even so very often defended and applied (Cohen, Hazelrigg, & Pope, 1975) view is proposed by Max
Weber (1922) who argues that different types of rationality exist,
each one driven by different factors. Rational social action is broken
down in purposive rationality (Zweckrationale) on the one hand and
value rationality (Wertrationale) on the other hand. Weber (1922)
sees purposive rational actions as actions to attain some end by the conscious and calculated use of certain means. Value-rational oriented actions he sees as actions driven by a conscious belief in the value for its
own sake of some ethical, aesthetic, or other form of behavior, no matter
of its prospects of success. In this, we translate Weber's Zweckrationale
into a rational approach of channel choice depending on how a channel
fits the task at hand best given its characteristics, in order to complete
the task as efficient and effective as possible. Examples of determinants
that relate to this approach are for example channel characteristics, task
characteristics, and the nature of the interaction (Reddick, 2005; Ebbers
et al., 2008; Reddick & Turner, 2012). Moreover, we translate Weber's

4.1. Task-related factors
Existing literature shows that channel choice is determined by rational determinants related to tasks. These include urgency, need for closure, the task at hand, and complexity, which will be discussed in
more detail below. In our view, and by following Weberian rationales,
urgency and need for closure do not relate to emotional behavior or
Affektuelles Handeln, but to Zweckrationale hence to task characteristics. Weber (1922) states that affectual social action goes beyond any rational behavior, while urgency and need for closure are related to a task

that needs to be dealt with in due time in order to successfully complete
the task. One could argue that in that case both determinants relate to
Wertrationale. However, completion of a task is a successfully pursued
result. Wertrationale, as Weber puts it, is “eines bestimmten
Sichverhaltens … unabhängig vom Erfolg” (see § 2. Grundriß der
Sozialökonomik III. Abteilung Wirtschaft und Gesellschaftor in English,
Weber, 1922) or in English: ‘certain behavior regardless of results’.
Hence our decision to place these variables under Zweckrationale.
Urgency, or time pressure, is the extent to which a task needs immediate attention by a citizen. Pieterson (2009) and Van den Boer (2014)
found time pressure to be a significant predictor of channel choice.
More specifically, if respondents faced time pressure, the more likely
they chose the telephone to contact the government (Pieterson, 2009).
Need for closure is the individual's need to complete the task at
hand. People with a high need for closure experience stress in uncertain
situations and therefore prefer to reduce that uncertainty by finishing
the given task as soon as possible. According to Pieterson (2009) the
preference for the front desk and telephone increases when people experience a high need for closure. Pieterson (2009) suggests that if the
need for closure is high, people tend to engage in a more rational decision making process.
The nature of the interaction related to the task is considered to have
an influence on channel choice (Pieterson & van Dijk, 2007; Reddick,
2005; Reddick & Turner, 2012). Research shows that if citizens just
need simple information to complete a task, they tend to use the
website (Australian Government, 2005; Pieterson, 2009). Citizens who
need a more complex problem to be solved are more likely to use the
phone or the front desk (Pieterson, 2009; Pieterson & Ebbers, 2008;
Reddick, 2005; Reddick, 2010). Research studies in other settings has
also confirmed the role of task complexity as a driver of channel choice
(Black, Lockett, Ennew, Winklhofer, & McKechnie, 2002; Lee, 2002).
We operationalize nature of the interaction related to the task twofold: the ‘task at hand’ and ‘task complexity’. The tasks at hand could be
a task related to actions such as garbage disposal, obtaining subsidy, relocation or municipal care. As mentioned previously, task complexity is
about the amount of information that needs to be transferred in relation
to a certain task. Task complexity is also related to the subjective uncertainty about the task inputs, process, and outcomes (Byström & Järvelin,
1995; Ebbers & Van Dijk, 2007; Van de Ven & Ferry, 1980). Although it is
clear that channel choice is directly influenced by complexity, it is less
clear, under which circumstances people perceive a situation to be complex, e.g., what factors influence complexity. Therefore, this research
unravels the role of complexity. In other words, it investigates which
of the other task related factors (i.e. the task at hand) determine the
complexity of a situation. In light of the reasoning above, complexity
was included in the research model as a mediator between the ‘the
task at hand’, urgency, and need for closure on the one hand and channel choice on the other hand.

4.3. Habit
Research indicates that the process of choosing is strongly influenced by habits (Pieterson, 2009). In his qualitative research,
Pieterson (2009) found that some people were used to call the government, others to visit the website or to go to the front desk.
Kræmmergaard and Østergaard Madsen's (2015) work on changing behaviors suggests that habits play a role in how people select channels
and they recommend ‘piggybacking’ existing habits in channel
strategies.
4.4. Personal characteristics
Many studies have shown that the basic personal characteristics,
such as age, education and gender are predictors of channel choice
(Ebbers et al., 2008; Ebbers & van Dijk, 2007; Pieterson & van Dijk,
2007; Reddick, 2005). People who visit the website tend to be younger,
higher educated and male (Pieterson & Ebbers, 2008). Those who like to
contact the government at the front desk tend to be aged 50+ and educated at the secondary school level (Ebbers et al., 2008). People who
are older tend to use the phone to contact the government more frequently (Reddick, 2005).
All of the above can be summarized with the explorative research

5. Research method
5.1. Research method: Survey with vignettes
Channel choice is a decision making process and thus a behavioral process. As such, regular survey methods do not suffice for
several reasons. First, regular surveys are best used to measure
opinions and perceptions about behavior instead of measuring
real behavior. Second, surveys are deficient when it comes to the
richness of details on decision making when compared to observations. Moreover, one cannot make inferences on behavior based
on surveys to the same extent as one can on experiments. Therefore,
a scenario-based (vignette) method was used for this survey.
Scenario-based studies enable the identification of separate influences on the many factors that go into such judgment and choices
(Rossi & Anderson, 1982). Within the vignette study approach,
respondents are confronted with short scenarios, also referred to
as vignettes (Morrison, Stettler, & Anderson, 2004), in which different variables are manipulated.
By reading vignettes respondents are more involved in the situation
compared to questionnaires and therefore the choices in real life are
better reflected (Karren & Barringer, 2002; van de Wijngaert &
Bouwman, 2011). This approach enables assessment of the importance
of various variables or factors to decision making. In total, 48 unique vignettes were designed. An overview of the variables and manipulations

Sampling and fieldwork were carried out by a professional research

5.4. Data analysis
We used the program Warp PLS version 5 (Kock, 2015), to build the
structural equation models (SEMs). The partial least squares path
modeling method to structural equation modeling allows estimating
complex cause-effect relationship models with latent variables (Hair,
Hult, Ringle, & Sarstedt, 2014; Vinzi, Trinchera, & Amato, 2010). For Partial Least Squares (PLS) analysis it is not necessary for variables to be
normally distributed (Chin, 1998).
In the analysis we chose the service that scored lowest on channel
choice within a channel as the baseline dummy. For the Front desk
and Website this was Reporting Public Space and for Telephone this
was Passport (see Table 3). This choice facilitates interpretation of the
betas related to the service characteristics.
The tested models all met the model fit and quality indices provided
by WarpPLS. The p-values of the Average Path Coefficient (APC), Average R-Squared (ARS), and Average Adjusted R-squared (AARS) of the
model were calculated through a process of resampling estimations
coupled with corrections in order to counter the standard error compression effects that are associated with adding random variables. All
p-values of these measures were significant at the 0.05 level. All measures and evaluation criteria can be found in Appendix 1 (combined
loadings and cross-loadings of Front desk, Telephone and Website for
multi-item latent constructs) and Appendix 2 (WarpPLS model fit and
most important quality indices for Front desk, Telephone and Website).
Due to the fact that each respondent was presented with four situations,
the data has a multi-level structure. The effect might be that the p-value
of a given relationships that was tested is underestimated. Because
WarpPLS does not allow multi-level modeling, we chose the conservative significance level of 0.01 to interpret the final results to overcome
this issue. The main results of the structural model are shown in Table 6.

6. Results
Variables that were not suitable to measure within the vignettes
were asked in usual survey questions. These include habit, prior experiences, self-efficacy, trust in government and socio-demographic variables (age, education and gender). To measure habit, a scale was used
with 10 items for each channel, such as ‘how often do you call to friends
and family to keep in touch’ and ‘how often do you call to contact governmental organizations’ for the telephone and ‘how often do you use
the Internet to look up governmental information’ and ‘how often do
you use the Internet for social network sites, such as Facebook?’. The
scale consisted of 7 points (1 = never, 7 = every day). The scale's reliability was α = 0.79 for telephone and α = 0.71 for Internet
(Cronbach's alpha). Satisfaction was measured in two ways, by asking
for an overall channel grade, ranging from 1 to 10 and asking for the satisfaction with regard to the most popular channel amongst our respondents (telephone) with 5 items, such as ‘the employee I spoke with was
friendly’ and ‘I was satisfied about the contact’. The scale's reliability was
α = 0.96. Computer self-efficacy was measured with 6 items, such as ‘I
think it is easy to use the Internet’ and ‘I know a lot about the use of the
Internet’. The scale's reliability was α = 0.91.

the application for a subsidy is significantly correlated to perceived

task is correlated to complexity for all three channels. For Need for Closure all relationships are non-significant.
Perceived complexity has a significant positive effect on channel
choice for front desk and telephone. If citizens perceive a situation to
be complex they tend to choose the front desk and the telephone.
We also see a direct relation between the choice for a channel and
the nature of the task. Urgency also directly influences channel choice;
the more citizens perceive a situation to be urgent, the more likely it is
they choose for the telephone. Further, all direct relations for Need for
Closure are non-significant.
In conclusion, the telephone is the most popular channel amongst
citizens. Based on the structural model testing, it can be concluded
that the telephone becomes even more popular when something is different than usual, for instance, when a situation is perceived to be more
complex. A possible explanation is that people easily believe a situation
to be complex. This is in line with social influence model. Situations can
be perceived to be complex based on the task at hand or urgency. For
the Internet channel this means that citizens only use the Internet
when a situation is very specific and non-complex.

7. Main conclusions and discussion
A growing list of studies has been conducted in the past decade in
order to understand eGovernment-related channel choice. Many studies suggest that besides rational determinants also many less than purely rational and even irrational determinants play a role. However, few
studies exist that combine these different elements in one study. For
that reason, in this study we moved past pure rational channel choice
and incorporated factors that are less rational and even irrational in
one model.
Our study found that both rational and irrational determinants are
important, as both determine channel choices by citizens in
eGovernment. This study also found that complexity is significant for
predicting channel choice, but not for predicting the choice of Website.
Instead, complexity is a predictor for Telephone and Front desk. Furthermore, the findings are largely in line with Pieterson's (2009). Habit is a
strong predictor of channel choice, but in certain situations and for certain tasks do people make choices deviating from the patterns predicted
by their habits. This suggests that certain variables trigger a more or less
conscious decision making process. Factors such as urgency and complexity act as such triggers.
Moreover, despite the strong role of habit, the nature of the service
also influences which channels people choose. This effect is strongest
for face-to-face communication. This might be due to the fact that people generally have to put more effort into utilizing face-to-face

7.1. Channel choice research
In capturing the contribution of this study above and formulating future research, we feel that there are three main points of criticism that
need to be dealt with regarding channel choice research as well as (partially) this study.
First of all, most channel choice studies are quantitative and typically
incorporate only a limited set of determinants (like ours). While we feel
our study makes important contributions to the field by going to a more
fundamental level of explaining behavioral processes and combining
several of these processes in one model, we do realize our model is far
from complete. As previous studies (see above) have included and
found positive causal relationships of many more factors on channel
choice, our study is limited in not including all factors. Furthermore,
with so many different studies and variables available, the question
arises whether we do not need more qualitative (and theoretical)
work to fully understand the processes that drive channel choice.
Second, convergence of (new) media is still accelerating. Computermediated communication is no long merely text and figures. Computermediated communication nowadays is also about audio and visual cues
enabled by messengers programs, webchat or microblogs and the like.
In the near future we think that a division between for instance telephone and website does not really make sense any more. In practice
(see e.g. European Commission, 2014) we see convergence or blending
of service channels being implemented. This could very well change the
way we ought to define channels and conduct research on channel behavior. A more blended and changing service landscape where the rigidity of thinking in terms of separate channels has to be replaced with a
vision that outlines how channels can be configured based on a set of
available properties.
In our opinion, future eGovernment channel choice studies should
use the combination of both types of channel choice behavior and
focus on studying more precisely what makes citizens choose what
type of behavior under what circumstances. Questions that follow on
our research are for instance ‘what instances transform habitual channel
choice processes into rational choice processes to what result?’

the properties to deal with this task. This is a view not too dissimilar
from communication theories such as Media Richness Theory (Daft &

While the Ebbers et al. (2008) model argues that complexity is a key
driver of channel choices in general, our study found that complexity is
no significant predictor related to whether or not citizens choose the
Website channel. This can only partially be explained from the original
thinking of MCM model of Ebbers et al.: more complex tasks create
more ambiguity, which renders stronger preferences for richer media
(Ebbers et al., 2008, Pieterson, 2009). As such, it does not come as a surprise that complexity does not predict website preferences. However, it
was also noticed that complexity is a stronger predictor for telephone
channel than for the front desk. Theoretically, when following rational
paradigms of channel choice such as Ebbers et al. (2008) it should be
the other way around. Empirically, this is not the case.
Furthermore, and more importantly, few channel and communication models include the notion that behavior is not necessarily driven
my rational decision making processes. In that sense, there are few theoretical insights about how irrational of habitual behavior impact the
choice of communication channels, let alone what the implication is of
this insight for the design and configuration of multi-channel service
strategies.
This, combined with the point about blending channels, calls for a review of existing service channel models and revised or new models.
Our study also has practical contributions. First of all, the above fosters that strategies to increase the use of digital channels could go two
ways; either develop habits for users towards using the preferred channel in all situations. Convergence of channels or blending (see European
Commission, 2014) could allow for channels to be richness or characteristic agnostic and be configured based on a set of available components.
In this situation, you can just push people online and take it from there.
A second and perhaps more short term approach is to increase the
awareness of users about their choice situation and educate them
about the suitability of channels for certain tasks. This falls in line with
the idea that through channel marketing (see Teerling & Pieterson,
2010), citizens can be steered towards certain channels. Based on our
work, we recommend that channel marketing initiatives focus more
on disrupting current and creating new habits. In that sense, channel
marketing should focus more on persistence (in creating those habits)
instead of just causing people to change channels once.

Second, we found that people are able to distinguish between the
different channels and choose rationally (both Zweckrationale and
Wertrationale), depending on task characteristics and related channel
preferences, but people also choose habitually. The strong role of habits
in its turn is a plea for a tight integration between channels. If people are
really motivated by their habits, which becomes increasingly clear, we
need to (per Kræmmergaard and Østergaard Madsen (2015) suggestion) piggyback on their behaviors and work from there. This implies
citizens have to be seamlessly routed to their destination if they enter
the system at a ‘wrong’ point. To some extent though, situational factors
can be built into governmental channel strategies. Urgency will very
likely correlate with deadlines and time left to meet a deadline. This is
all information that could be part of the channel design. A citizen who
has lots of time left could be guided to self-help channels whereas a citizen with little time left can be guided straight to the phone.
Several limitations needs to be mentioned. The first is the exclusion
of emotions from this work, despite the finding in the literature that
emotions play an important role. As explained above, we feel that we
could not incorporate emotions in our research design. Therefore, future
studies should address the role of emotions in channel choice processes
in more detail. Another limitation concerns the generalizability of the
vignette findings. Vignettes allow us to examine factors predicting
channel preferences under tightly controlled conditions that are free
of other factors that complicate research in real-life settings. That is
their main strength and their main weakness. However, the artificiality
of the experimental setting may limit the applicability of these results to
the real world. As we already mentioned above, we believe it is about
time to conduct a more qualitative method, one that anticipates the
ever accelerating conversion of computer mediated communication
channels and traditional channels like telephone and front desk.
