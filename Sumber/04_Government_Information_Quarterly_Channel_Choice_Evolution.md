### ABSTRACT

Keywords:
Channel behavior

Multi-channel management
Channel choice
eGovernment
Public service delivery

Governments and citizens alike are reaching maturity levels in their Internet adoption. With citizens using online
channels for more and more purposes, governments around the world are moving to a ‘digital by default’ service
delivery strategy. The question arises as to whether online channels are indeed suited for all purposes and are the
“go-to” channels when citizens need information in a variety of situations. In this study, we compare data
collected in 2008 and 2017 in the Netherlands to assess the evolution in channel behavior. The analysis suggests
that channel preferences and general usage are indeed shifting towards online channels, whilst gaps in online
usage between age groups are narrowing. However, the findings also show that this shift is not uniform across all
tasks. For certain purposes, choices for traditional channels remain surprisingly resilient. The findings suggest
that while online channels are maturing, they are still not ready to replace other existing channels for all types of
services. Our findings call for continued attention to integrated channel strategies that incorporate the strengths
of individual service channels.

1. Introduction
In the Netherlands, citizens have a wide variety of channels at their
disposal to enable them to contact government agencies if they need to
use services or ask questions. Commonly, citizens can go online to find
information at government websites; they can call a government
agency; visit an agency in person; write an e-mail or letter; and, more
recently, use social media. However, the online channel landscape is
not stable. Since the arrival of the Internet and associated service
channels (most notably websites and e-mail), governments have started
to move their services online (van Deursen & Pieterson, 2006) and this
has been gradually followed by an uptake of these services by citizens.
Online channels have grown so important that they are becoming
governments' channel of choice for service delivery. The European
Union, for example, is explicitly pursuing a “digital by default” strategy
in which the online channels form the core of service strategies
(European Union, 2017). The question, of course, arises as to whether
such a strategy is wise. The multi-channel management literature is
occupied with finding answers to the question of how governments
should manage and position their service channels in order to deliver
services most effectively and efficiently (Madsen & Hofmann, 2019).
Most of the existing models or frameworks within this literature assign
certain properties to service channels and argue that these differences

However, these frameworks assume that channels and services have
a fixed set of characteristics that do not change. In this way they follow,
and often build upon, communication (media) theories to help understand differences between channels (see Pieterson, 2010, for a discussion), their appropriateness for certain service tasks and how citizens
choose and use these channels. Most prominent of these theories is
Media Richness Theory (Daft & Lengel, 1986; also see Madsen &
Kræmmergaard, 2015a and Pieterson, 2009 for overviews). The theory
argues that media have fixed properties and their different characteristics render them suitable for different types of tasks. Other perspectives in communication theory are also relevant: a prominent example
is Channel Expansion Theory (Carlson & Zmud, 1999), which argues
that media properties are not fixed but rather socially constructed
through experiences. As people gain experiences by using channels,
they are able to use them for a broader, more complicated, set of tasks.

2.2. Previous studies on channel behavior
One of the most recent empirical publications on channel behavior
is that of Ebbers, Jansen, Pieterson, and van de Wijngaert (2016). Their
study focuses primarily on channel choice and the process underlying
this choice. They found that the process can be a more or less rational
decision-making process, depending on the task at hand, the personal
characteristics of the citizen and situational factors. Pieterson (2009)
also argued that the type of channel choice decision-making can be
driven by habitual (non-rational) behavior rather than a more deliberate decision-making process. The study found that different services
lead to different channel choices, e.g. citizens requiring a new passport
are more likely to use an in-person channel (front desk) while people
with a waste related enquiry are more likely to use the phone. As for the
underlying task characteristics, they found that the complexity of the
task is an important predictor for choice and the same applies to the
urgency of the situation: the more urgent the situation, the more likely
a citizen is to choose the telephone (regardless of the task at hand).
Ebbers, Jansen, and van Deursen (2016) conducted another empirical study on channel behavior, with an aim of linking channel
choice and usage to the digital divide. Their study shows that digital
skills do predict the degree of satisfaction: the more digitally skilled
citizens are, the more satisfied they are with online services. Results
also show that the nature of interaction significantly coheres with
channel choice. Furthermore, they found age to be a predictor of
channel choice in general, with mixed results for education and gender.
This role of demographic factors may differ per country. Reddick and
Anthopoulos (2014), for instance, found that females and people of
higher age in Canada were less likely to use electronic channels. In their
study on channel choice in Canada, education proved not significant.

A German study, in the context of public employment services

choices for the different services. For example, 43% of respondents

(under 40) and more highly educated citizens gravitating more towards

Kræmmergaard researched how the introduction of mandatory online
services impacted the use of the online channel, as well as the backup
and secondary telephone channel. Their main findings were that: a) the
obligatory use of online channels drastically increased adoption of the
online channels, and b) the increased use of online channels did not by
itself lead to efficiency gains, because many customers kept using traditional channels. This was largely due to: c) the poor communication
around online channels leading to increased pressure on the traditional
channels.
This aligns with recent findings from the private sector. Seck and
Philippe (2013) found that there was a change in consumer behavior
towards the combined use of the different channels offered by a single
service provider. These so-called ‘multi-channel customers’ used different channels for different purposes, often at the same time, and expected to have a uniform and seamless quality of experience across
multiple channels. This finding has implications when it comes to
customer satisfaction. Some studies have focused on customer satisfaction in a multi-channel environment, including all channels used
by customers; for an overview, see Seck and Philippe (2013). Across
these studies, it appears that customer satisfaction is impacted by the
quality of all channels. Hence, lower quality service in one channel will
impact on customer satisfaction across all channels in use.
All in all, the empirical studies on over time changes in channel
choice behaviors teach us that: a) the use of online channels in most

countries is on the rise, resulting in a decline in the use of traditional
channels, and in some countries this process is faster than in others; b)
over time, the use of online channels could stabilize or even regress
(such as in Canada); c) the use of channels in general could be motivated by many factors, including the fit between service and channel
but also the supply of services, quality of service delivery or even the
mandatory nature of certain interactions (such as in Denmark). The
different settings in the few studies comparing changes over time make
it complicated to assess the influence of the various variables and to
isolate the effect of independent variables. This, naturally, also complicates channel choice scholars' abilities to make strong theoretical
claims about those variables in order to explain the evolution of the
channel choice behaviors. However, the most important theories in this
field will be discussed below.
2.3. Theories on channel choice
From a theoretical perspective, scholars since the 1970s have been
occupied with the questions of how and why people choose and use
certain channels to exchange information, mostly in relation to the
properties of these channels (or media). While different media theories,
including Media Richness Theory and Channel Expansion Theory, are
frequently cited in the channel choice literature (for an overview, see
Madsen & Kræmmergaard, 2015), few empirical studies incorporate all
elements of these theories and no studies exist that focus on over time
changes in channel behaviors in the context of these theories.
Media Richness Theory (Daft & Lengel, 1986) is probably the bestknown theory in this field. The theory argues that media have a (fixed)
set of properties that render them suitable for different types of tasks.
Daft and Lengel (1986) describe four media properties: i) immediacy of
feedback; ii) level of personalization; iii) number of cues used by the
medium (e.g., audio, video); and iv) the level of language variety offered by the medium. Media scoring high in richness (e.g., having many
cues and allowing personalization and language variety) are then more
suited to solving ambiguous communication problems, whereas it
would be inefficient to use rich media for simple, unequivocal tasks.
Several theories have built on this notion of task-medium fit, with
varying assumptions regarding the properties of media and tasks.
Contrasting views also exist: for example, Channel Expansion Theory
(Carlson & Zmud, 1999) argues that properties of media are flexible and
evolve with users' experiences. This suggests that people who use a
particular medium more frequently gain the skills to utilize these
channels for more ambiguous tasks. These different theoretical perspectives are often said to be conflicting, creating either a dichotomy
between more rational versus social approaches towards media
(Pieterson, 2009) or two ends of a scale with various other theoretical
perspectives sitting in the middle (Fernandez, Simo, Sallan, & Enache,
2013). Empirical research on this matter is divided and, within the
context of citizen-to-government interaction and service delivery, few
empirical studies exist that focus on all elements of these theories or
compare contrasting theories. Pieterson, Teerling, and Ebbers (2008)
studied citizens' channel perceptions and their conclusions were mixed;
they found that channel perceptions did vary strongly across different
demographic groups. However, they also found that certain perceptions
were strongly tied to specific types of channels (such as face-to-face
interactions being seen as a rich channel by all groups). Similarly,
Pieterson (2009) found results supporting both ends of the spectrum,
with situation being the factor that dictated whether citizens were more
or less rational in their channel choice behaviors and perceptions of
channels. The situation becomes more ambiguous when considering
changes in perceptions and channel choice behaviors over time. While
Channel Expansion Theory suggests that experience influences channel
perceptions, very few studies exist studying these changes over time. In
the context of more general communication, studies suggest that the
channel expansion effect does apply, but this effect might be compounded by the general acceptance and use of communication media

Existing multi-channel models of governmental service delivery

channel properties when describing how certain channels could fit
certain services. Ebbers et al. (2008), for example, argue that two
properties of tasks exist: the first is task complexity, which they define
as “the extent of multiple interrelated actions that have to be taken to
solve one problem” (p. 191); the second is task ambiguity which they
define as “the existence of multiple and conflicting interpretations
about a situation” (p. 191). Furthermore, they match these task properties to channels that need to be used for these tasks. For example, in
situations with low complexity and high ambiguity they recommend
using the telephone. Wirtz and Langer (2017) take a similar approach.
They map a total of fifteen channels on their cost/benefit ratio, communication capacity and service provision capacity scales. Based on this
mapping, they connect the channels to different types of customer
touchpoints (such as information, communication, and transactions).
Both frameworks treat the characteristics as fixed properties of channels and tasks and thus adopt a perspective similar to Media Richness
Theory. As a consequence, these frameworks lose (part) of their usefulness if channel (and task) characteristics are not fixed and objectively assessable, but rather subjective and based on perceptions and
experiences.

3.1. Aggregated channel choices and channel preferences
The first set of questions concerns aggregated choices and channel
preferences. The former refers to the question of which channel citizens
report using most frequently when interacting with governments. This
gives us an idea of the channels used in general, from the perspective of
the citizen, regardless of the task or situation at hand. Secondly, we
asked about citizens' channel preferences, i.e., the channel they like to
use when interacting with governments. Any difference between this
‘use’ and ‘preference’ could be explained by many factors, including
supply not matching demand (for example, a citizen would like to use
one channel, but this service can only be obtained via another channel);
mandatory use of a channel (e.g., a citizen would like to use one
channel, but has to use another) or experiences (for instance, a citizen
would like to use one channel, but has learned that another channel
yields a better outcome).
3.2. Demographic variables
The second group of questions relates to the demographic variables
of age, gender and education. Many studies have shown these are potentially important predictors of channel choice, with the discussion
above highlighting how previous research has been inconclusive about
the effects of these variables.
3.3. Types of tasks and situations
The third and final group of questions focus on tasks and situations.
These break down into two different sub-types: a) questions about the
characteristics of tasks or services and b) the different situations in
which channel choices take place which could trigger different channel
choice behaviors. Previous research (for overviews see Madsen &
Kræmmergaard, 2015a; Pieterson, 2009) shows that all of these variables are important for channel choices. Another reason to include
them is that due to their highly subjective nature, as argued by Channel
Expansion Theory, we would expect to see changes in channel choices
over time for these variables.
The first sub-type consists of specific task-related characteristics.
Services consist typically of different components, and different parts of
the service delivery process require different actions. Previous studies
(Ebbers et al., 2008; Pieterson, 2009) also make the distinction between
task type and task characteristics; they found that channel choices for
certain tasks could differ based on a different interpretation of the
characteristics of said task. Two variables stand out as being included
most frequently (see Madsen & Kræmmergaard, 2015a): the complexity
and ambiguity of tasks. The complexity of tasks is probably the variable
included in most studies (e.g., Ebbers, Jansen, & van Deursen, 2016;
Mundy, Umer, & Foster, 2011; Pieterson, 2009) and, following the
definition given above, refers to the number of interrelated steps
needed to complete a task (Ebbers et al., 2008). Studies in general find
that as complexity increases, people are more likely to choose traditional channels (Ebbers, Jansen, Pieterson, & van de Wijngaert, 2016),
despite the argument from Ebbers et al. (2008) that online channels are
better at reducing complexities (e.g., through wizards). Because of the
prominence of complexity in the literature, we have included two
permutations of this variable: ‘simple questions’ and ‘complex questions’. Ambiguity has to do with meaning (Pieterson, 2009) and not
being sure of the exact nature of the task or problem needs to be assessed (Ebbers et al., 2008).
The second sub-type consists of the characteristics of the situation.
Firstly, the urgency of the situation is key. As a result of lack of time or
urgency, people will alter the level of rationality applied in their decision-making process, resulting in different channel choice behaviors.
According to Payne, Bettman, and Johnson (1988), this depends on the
desired accuracy of the decision-making process and the desire to
minimize the (cognitive) effort required to make this decision.

2.4. Research questions
The discussion of existing research above confirms that there are no
studies comparing shifts in channel choices over (longer) periods in
time, across many different channels, for different tasks, and comparing
multiple (demographic) groups. Furthermore, the overview of channel
choice theories and multi-channel frameworks suggests different outcomes depending on the theoretical approach. The aim of this study is
to reduce some of these ambiguities by conducting an empirical study
of channel choices and uses over time, across different channels, tasks
and situations, and different demographic groups. The following research questions guide the research:
− RQ1. What are the general shifts over time in usage and preferences
of information channels for interacting with government?
− RQ2. What are the shifts in channel choices for specific tasks and/or
situations?
− RQ3. How do channel choices and preferences differ between demographic groups?
3. Method
For this study, we compare data collected in 2008 (published,
among others in Pieterson, 2009) and data collected in 2017. For the
2017 data collection, exactly the same questions from the 2008 study
were used. These have been published and were reused from the original publication (Pieterson, 2009). In both cases, these questions
formed part of a broader and longer questionnaire. The following types
of questions were included and will be discussed below.
− Questions about aggregated channel choices and channel preferences.
− Questions about specific channel choices for specific tasks and situations.
− Demographic variables.

United Nations eGovernment Survey ranked the Netherlands fifth in the
world in its eGovernment Development Index (after Sweden, Denmark
Norway and the US). In the 2018 survey, the Netherlands had dropped
to the 13th spot on this index, with the caveat that most (western)
countries have converged in the past 10 years. In its supply of services,
the country has made much progress in the past decade. According to a
study by Deloitte, around 75% of all government services were available online in 2017, rising from 58% in, 2014 (Zarbanoui, Gianolio, &
Eikenboom, 2017). While data from 2008 is lacking, it is safe to assume
that online availability of services was well below 50% in 2008.
Similarly, on the demand side of online services, things have
changed as well. According to Eurostat, 86% of all Dutch households
had access to the Internet in 2008 and in 2017 this figure was 98%. For
broadband, the jump is from 74% to 98% and for mobile access it is
from 55% (in 2012) to 87% in 2017.
With the rise of online channels and information provided on
websites came changes in the provision of other channels. As the government has moved information online, much less information is being
offered on paper and the ‘written’ channel is becoming obsolete.
However, as documented in Pieterson (2009), most of the obsolescence
of the written channel happened prior to 2008. The past 10 years did
see a reduction in the number of physical locations of many government
agencies. However, while the number of separate offices went down,
the availability of in-person contact remained relatively stable, for example because government agencies started using shared service locations. Municipal offices now often form a gateway to many other government agencies and allow citizens to request information in person.
Changes in telephone and e-mail channels happened largely prior to
2008. The Dutch national government launched the program
“Antwoord©” (Answer©) in 2006 in an effort to bundle channels in
customer contact centers. Inspired by the ‘311’ concept in the US, the
government started creating single points of entry (e.g., one phone

closure on a matter that is of higher importance to them. The role of
importance (and NfC) has been researched in the past in the government domain (Ebbers, Jansen, Pieterson, & van de Wijngaert, 2016;
Pieterson, 2009; van den Boer, Pieterson, Arendsen, & van Dijk, 2017)
with results typically suggesting that higher importance leads to a
choice for personal (e.g., via telephone) communication. This variable
was included in two ways within our questions: asking about channel
choices in ‘important situations’, pointing to the role of importance and
when ‘certainty is needed’ referring to the role of need for closure.
Lastly, we include situational factors that could trigger different
channel choice decision-making processes. These were included following the work of Pieterson (2009), who found that citizens, when
choosing channels, either follow a conscious process of rational decision-making based on an elaboration of task-channel fit, or an unconscious process of habitual decision-making. In this process they
simply pick a channel without thinking, based on their experiences.
Madsen and Kræmmergaard (2016) found further evidence of the role
of habits in channel choice decisions, especially in situations that
people are familiar with. Furthermore, Madsen and Kræmmergaard
(2015a, b) work on behavioral change suggests that habits play an
important role in the process of channel choices. Lastly, Ebbers, Jansen,
Pieterson, and van de Wijngaert (2016) also found that habits are a
primary driver of channel choice behaviors. Within the context of this
study, a key question is whether channel behaviors have shifted over
time and whether this shift has affected habits. For this reason, we
included the question regarding which channel citizens would choose
‘without thinking’. While we realize this is a far from perfect operationalization, it might shed some light on the matter (Table 1).

3.5. Sample and respondents
In both rounds of data collection, research panels were used to select respondents and in both cases panel members were randomly invited to participate in the studies. In both studies, a research agency
was asked to randomly invite participants from their panel and invite
sufficient numbers of participants to guarantee an even spread of participants across the different demographic groups to ensure a reliable
sample for the Dutch population. For the 2017 study, the instructions
for the research agency regarding sampling from the 2008 study were
re-used and changed to fit the time.
In the 2008 study, a total of n = 2442 citizens completed the
survey, whereas in 2017 n = 1237 completed the questionnaire. Thus,
the 2008 survey has almost twice the number of respondents.
Furthermore, this (2008) survey was not entirely representative of the
Dutch population in terms of the spread of respondents across all demographic groups. For example, higher educated citizens as well as the
older age groups were over represented. In the original study [redacted
for review purposes] this was solved by weighing the various demographic groups differently. However, for the purposes of this (comparative) study, this is not an ideal solution. The most important reason
is the combination of the differences in sample size with the nature of
the types of analyses we were conducting (mostly chi-square tests). Chisquare tests are known to be sensitive to sample size and thus the differences in sample sizes between the two samples could lead to (unwanted) different outcomes. Weighing groups could compound this
issue.
To solve this problem of sample size and differences in distribution,
we chose to sub-sample the 2008 data set. Using R, we randomly
sampled from within each (demographic) group, but varied the probability of inclusion based on the degree of over- or under-representation
(using the weights from the original study). This increased the likelihood that under-represented groups were chosen in the sub-sample
and decreased the likelihood that over-represented groups were chosen.
We set the sub-sample size at approximately 50% and ended up with a
final sample of n = 1229 respondents for the 2008 study, which is
nearly the same size as the 2017 sample and comparable in distribution.

across rows and columns.
2. Columns pairwise Secondly, we conduct separate Chi-square tests

3. Rows pairwise The third step is similar to the second, but for rows

5. Discussion

4.3. Tasks and situations

This study shows how channel behaviors are changing over time. In
general, we are seeing the shift from traditional service channels to
electronic channels in the Netherlands and we are seeing convergence
in channel behaviors between the different demographic groups. A big
part of this shift is due to the rise of the electronic channels and, in part,
the reduction of the availability of certain traditional channels (mostly
the in-person and written channels). However, under the surface, the
situation is less straightforward. Despite the general shift in overall
usage and preference, on a task/situation level, shifts are happening for
certain tasks, but not for others.
The finding that the overall channel shift and evolution in channel
behavior is happening in the Netherlands is no real surprise. While
Internet access was already nearing saturation levels in the Netherlands
in 2008, the supply of electronic services by governments was not
nearly as saturated and has grown strongly in the past decade. The shift
in overall usage towards electronic channels mirrors this growth in
supply of online services. This does suggest that increasing the number
of services for citizens online, combined with high levels of Internet
access, will stimulate the uptake of online channels and may reduce the
use of traditional channels.
Our study contributes in two ways to the domain of eGovernment
studies. First, it adds to the field of channel choice research and theory
in this area in general. Second, and more specifically, it creates implications for future work that can incorporate the impact on governments caused by offering a multitude of channels. We will discuss both
briefly.

Expansion Theory suggests that channel capabilities grow with experience and we should therefore see channels being chosen for all tasks

Our findings suggest that elements of both are true. While in general

theories. In this way, although experience will change channel capabilities, channels do appear to have a certain baseline set of characteristics. This calls for renewed theory development in this area and
theories that explore the degree to which media have, perhaps, a set of
baseline features that are fixed and certain properties that are variable
and could change over time, for example based on experiences.

1. New channel management models need to be developed that allow
for more flexible interpretations of channel characteristics and
combine certain baseline (fixed) properties with certain flexible
elements that can change. This hinges largely on the incorporation
of more situational factors (also see Madsen & Hofmann, 2019;
Pieterson, 2009) that could influence channel perceptions and taskchannel fit. Furthermore, these models need to account for differences between groups of citizens and address how different groups
react differently in different situations.
2. Multi-channel management models need to be updated regularly to
reflect changes in the channel and services landscape. The integrated multi-channel framework of Ebbers et al. (2008), for example, is still being cited frequently, but is now showing its age; the
findings of this work challenge some of the assumptions of their
framework. This calls for frameworks that incorporate elements
pertaining to updates (e.g., making user research an element of the
model) and/or frameworks that are being updated more frequently.
Furthermore, there is a multitude of different types of channels
being deployed and the numbers keep rising (Pieterson, Ebbers, &
Madsen, 2017; Wirtz & Langer, 2017); this not only influences channel
management models, but also research into channel behaviour. While
in our study many new channels (e.g., social media) hardly played a
role, this situation could change drastically in the future.
For practitioners, our findings indicate that even decades after
Internet-based ‘electronic’ service channels were introduced to the
public, citizens are still frequently choosing traditional channels to
obtain information from governments. This challenges the digital-bydefault government strategies such as those propagated by the EU and
applied in countries such as the United Kingdom, Australia, and
Estonia. Our findings suggests such strategies do not align with the
behaviors and preferences of citizens. In a sense, our findings call for
care when implementing such strategies.

More important than the implications for communication theory,
given the nature of this study, are the implications for multi-channel
management models or frameworks. After all, these guide the implementation of governmental service delivery and channel strategies.
However, current multi-channel management models (e.g., Ebbers
et al., 2008; Wirtz & Langer, 2017) assume that channel characteristics
are fixed and are suited for a certain set of services, thereby assuming
there is an optimal task-channel fit.

6. Conclusions, limitations, and future work

6.1. Research limitations

The list of studies conducted in the past decade in order to understand e-government related channel preferences and channel choice, is
still growing. But despite this research attention, only a few studies
focus on the shift in preferences and choice over a longer period of time.
As of now, there are no studies on shifts in channel choices over longer
periods, across many different channels, for different tasks in different
situations, while comparing multiple demographic groups. In line with
this reasoning, we proposed three research questions to guide our
study:

There are some study limitations that we will now address. First of
all, both studies used data collected through the use of research panels
and respondents were recruited online. While most people in the
Netherlands have access to the Internet (at least in 2017), that was not
the case in 2008. Even in 2017 not the entire population is online or
uses the Internet frequently and will thus be part of an online panel.
Therefore, despite the careful selection of respondents and representation across all demographic groups, some bias could be present in the
results towards the online channels.
Secondly, the nominal nature of the data meant that we had to limit
ourselves to running Chi-square tests on the data. Chi-squares test for
independence, goodness of fit, and homogeneity at the same time
(Franke et al., 2012). However, Chi-square tests suffer from a number of
limitations: the first is the limited information the test yields. When
running a Chi-square, all we know is that the data are not evenly distributed across all categories. Beyond this we have very little information about the distribution of the data and any correlations or
causal relationships; this is called the omnibus effect. The second is that
Chi-squares are very sensitive to sample size; large samples, like ours
tend to yield many significant results and there are no easy ways to
correct for this. We have tried to control for this in two ways: firstly by
not solely focusing on the significance of the result, but also on the size
of the Chi-square value (relative to the sample size), secondly by also
considering the (relative) residuals that can help us interpret the
strength of the result.
Thirdly, while we are able to test for general evenness of distributions across two variables and run tests for distributions within variables, we are not able to combine the two and draw diagonal inferences.
For example, we are able to show that channel choices are not evenly
distributed for a situation in a certain year and we are able to show that
channel choices for each channel differ across years, but we are unable
to directly relate an increase in the choice for a channel in a situation
between years to a decrease in the choice for another channel. As such,
many of the inferences in channel shifts, while plausible, are indirect. In
order to verify our results, we can think of two alternative approaches:
one option is a true longitudinal study where shifts can be mapped
directly to the respondent. The second is the use of ordinal or scaled
variables that allow for more sophisticated means and variance based
testing.
Fourthly, our study focused on interactions between citizens and
governments with an informational nature, such as finding information
online or calling an agency with questions. Services with a more
transactional nature, such as filling out forms, filing applications, or
exchanging money, have not been part of this study and should be included in future work.
Lastly, as mentioned throughout this article, the study took place in
the Netherlands, a country with high levels of Internet penetration and
currently a relatively high maturity of online service offerings. While
we believe the channel shifts taking place in the Netherlands will also
happen in other countries over time, it remains uncertain to what extent
that will happen. Future research should assess the channel shifts in
other countries and comparative studies could focus on country-specific
factors (such as culture) that might explain any difference between
countries. These studies could also include more of the contextual
factors, such as the design and availability of online services. This information was not available for the purposes of this study. While we
have tried to describe the situation in the Netherlands as thoroughly as
possible, potentially important factors might have been missed.

1. What are the general shifts over time in usage and preferences of
channels for interacting with government?
2. What are the shifts in channel choices for specific tasks and/or situations?
3. How do channel choices and preferences differ between demographic groups?
Regarding the first research question, our study found a significant
shift in the most often used channels over the course of a decade in the
Netherlands. This shift happens in different directions: respondents
have changed which channels they use more frequently, but have not
swapped out one channel for another, rather the shift points to a more
general pattern of some ‘traditional’ channels gradually being replaced
with ‘electronic’ channels over a longer period of time. This is most
visible for in-person and written contacts, which we observe declining
across the board. This decline translate into gains for websites and
email as channel choices. Meanwhile, with regard to the telephone, the
situation is different and it suffers the least from this shift. Our study
also shows that, overall, channel preferences are shifting from traditional to electronic channels, but the results suggest that this shift is not
as strong as for channel usage.
As for the second research question, the answer is not straightforward. First of all, for nearly all tasks and situations, in line with the
above, in-person and written contacts are chosen less frequently in
2017 versus 2008. In all cases it appears that these channels have been
swapped for the electronic channels (website and email). Secondly, the
situation is more complicated for the telephone. For some of the tasks
(such as simple questions and non-urgent situations) there is a clear
shift in channel choices from the telephone to other choices (in this case
websites). Also, in line with the overall patterns of channel usage and
preferences, habits (‘without thought’) seem to be shifting in a similar
direction. However, for a number of tasks and situations there is no, or
a very small, shift in channel choices. This applies primarily for complex questions where there is virtually no shift and secondarily for situations of high urgency (‘quick response’), high importance and need
for closure (‘certainty needed’, ‘important question’) and for ambiguous
questions. Overall, it appears that two of the traditional channels are
being replaced by electronic channels. However, the telephone remains
as important in 2017 as it was in 2008. All in all, for 2017, three
channels (telephone, websites, and e-mail) account for most choices
and depending on the task or situation, distribution of choice varies
between these three.
In answering the third research question, the results show that regarding age, education and gender, the differences in channel usage
and preferences between the different demographic groups are converging. Whereas we found large differences for groups in all three
variables in their patterns of channel usage and preference in 2008,
these differences are much smaller in 2017. This pattern of convergence
is stronger for usage than for channel preferences. While this convergence is clearly happening, small yet significant differences do remain.
