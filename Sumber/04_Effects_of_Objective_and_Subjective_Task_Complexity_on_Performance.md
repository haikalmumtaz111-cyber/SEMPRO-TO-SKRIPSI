The thesis of this research is that performance on a task depends not only on objective
complexity (a task characteristic) but also on one's perception of task complexity.
This study allowed investigation of the influences of (a) cognitive ability, (b)
motivation, (c) subjective task complexity, and (d) task experience on performance
for an objectively simple or complex scheduling task. Potential determinants of
subjective complexity were also studied, as well as the mediating effects of subjective
complexity on the relations between independent variables (i.e., objective complexity, cognitive ability, and task experience) and task performance. Participants (N =
195) were undergraduates who prepared a 1-week work schedule for a fictitious film
processing store under either a Simple or Complex condition. Those in the Complex
condition were additionally required to account for employee "time off' and varying
store traffic. Hierarchical regression revealed significant, unique main effects for
objective task complexity and subjectivetask complexity in predicting task performance when controlling for cognitive ability. Subjective task complexity was found to
partially mediate the (a) objective task complexity-performance and (b) cognitive
ability-performance relations. Future directionsfor research and theory are discussed.
Task complexity has become an important variable in performance research,
particularly in regard to studies of goal setting (e.g., Campbell & Gingrich, 1986;

TASK COMPLEXITY IN GOAL-SElTING AND
DECISION-MAKING RESEARCH
Goal setting researchers have identified task complexity as a consistent moderator
of the goal-performance relation; numerous studies have demonstrated that the
positive effect of goal setting on task performance is stronger and more immediate
for simple rather than complex tasks (e.g., Earley, 1985; Wood, Mento, & Locke,
1987). However, there is presently no consensus on the processes underlying this
phenomenon. One hypothesis stems from the observation that the general success
of goal setting is due to increases in task motivation and effort to perform a task at
high levels (Campbell, 1991; Kernan, Bruning, & Miller-Guhde, 1994). Although
increases in effort normally translate into performance increases for simple tasks,
the relation between effort and performance for complex tasks may be weaker.
When task complexity is high, goals may only increase performance when the
individual becomes motivated to search for more efficient strategies for successful
task completion (Campbell, 1991).
Task complexity has also appeared in the decision-making literature,particularly
as a determinantof task strategy choice (e.g., Larichev & Moshkovich, 1988;Locke
et al., 1981).Under the guise of different names such as task performance strategies
(TPSs; Campbell, 1991) and task specificplans (TSPs; Wood & Locke, 1990),these
strategies are either created or recalled from memory to aid successful planning and
execution of a task to be performed. Research in this area has found that the task's
level of objective complexity determines which task strategy will be used (Bodenhausen & Lichtenstein, 1987; Paquette & Kida, 1988). For instance, in choice tasks
(such as choosing a car among many, given a list of attributes), compensatory
strategies are used if the task is simple, where all of the available information is
assessed for all possible alternatives.On the other hand, people performing complex
tasks depend first on a noncompensatory screening of choices (also called "elimination by aspects") using cutoffs on one or more chosen attributes (e.g., sticker
price, gas mileage) in order to minimize cognitive demands (e.g., Paquette & Kida,
1988).

HYPOTHESES
Potential Determinants of Task Performance
Objective task complexity has been found to decrease task performance for various
tasks such as work schedule creation (Campbell, 1984), course selection (Early,
1985), and "truck-routing" (Kernan et al., 1994). However, perceptions of complexity are likely to affect task performance independently of objective task
complexity,much as self-efficacy affects performance above and beyond the actual
abilities of the individual alone (Gist & Mitchell, 1992). It is possible that the
perception of a task being simple is tied to high levels of confidence in being able
to perform the task well, which in turn is related to higher levels of actual
performance. For example, Schroeder, Driver, and Streufert (1967) found that
different subjects experienced an identical task differently and those differences
were related to task performance.
People with higher levels of cognitive ability tend to perform better on the same
task than those with lower cognitive ability. Therefore, cognitive ability should
positively affect task performance. The effect of cognitive ability, however, should
not negate the unique influences of objective and subjective task complexity on
task performance. This assertion may be tested by treating cognitive ability as a
covariate in a hierarchical regression analysis.
Hypothesis la: Subjective task complexity will be significantly and nega-

tively related to task performance, even after the effects of objective task
complexity are accounted for, and objective task complexity will be significantly and negatively related to task performance, even after the effects of
subjective task complexity are accounted for. Furthermore, although cognitive ability should positively affect task performance, both types of complexity will predict task performance after controlling for cognitive ability.

to improve performance (Quifiones, Ford, & Teachout, 1995). In addition, motivation to perform well on a task has been linked to performance on that task; it has

Hypothesis Ib: Task experience, and task motivation, will each predict

Objective Task Complexity as a Moderator of
Performance Relations
A person asked to perform two similar tasks that differ strongly in objective task
complexity (e.g., painting a wall vs. painting a house) will likely draw on different
resources, such as task experience or cognitive ability, to perform each of those
tasks. Objective task complexity, then, may moderate relations between task
performance and person variables, such that the level of objective complexity
determines the strength (and possibly the direction) of the relation between independent variable and performance (see Baron & Kenny, 1986).
Cognitive ability has been found to correlate more highly with complex reaction
timelests than with simple ones (Larson, Merritt, &Williams, 1988). Furthermore,
decision-makingresearch and theory suggeststhat complex tasks require switching
between different strategies (Bodenhausen & Lichtenstein, 1987; Larichev &
Moshkovich, 1988; Paquette & Kida, 1988), as well as "selective trial-and-error"
(Simon, 1981), for organizing and prioritizing information; it is likely that these
actions require a great deal of mental effort.
Hypothesis 2a: Cognitive ability and objective task complexity will interact
to predict performance such that the relation between cognitive ability and
task performance will be stronger for objectively complex tasks than for
objectively simple tasks.

Strategy information has been found to increaseperformance for complex tasks but
not for simple ones (Earley, 1985). The best source of information for effective task
strategies is experience with that task (Bandura, 1977). Similarly, Simon (1981)
argued that the effectiveness of the selective trial-and-error strategy for complex
tasks depends partly on information or performance feedbackreceived through past
experience performing similar tasks.

The Task

A managerial scheduling task, similar to the one used and described by Campbell
(1984), was developed for the purpose of this study. Each participant created a
1-week schedule for a small fictitious film processing store with seven part-time
employees. The store guaranteed that any film dropped off at any time during the
week would be processed within 1 hr of being dropped off; if the prints were not
ready within 1 hr, the prints would be free of charge to the customer.This "one-hour
guarantee" policy was incorporated into the task so that there was a clear relation
between the number of employees scheduled and the net result (i.e., lost profit when
under-scheduling occurs).
Participants were told to create a schedule that would maximize the estimated
profit for the store. Participants were given information about (a) the hourly wages
of the employees, (b) the net profit for each roll of film developed on time, and (c)
how many rolls of film were expected to come into the store at each hour of the
store's business hours. Maximizing estimated profit required the participant to
judge how many employees were needed to process the amount of film expected
to come in to the store at a given time, and develop the schedule accordingly. If the
participant failed to schedule enough employees for a given hour, the result was a
loss of profit for those rolls of film not developed in time. On the other hand, the
store also lost profit (via wages) if the participant scheduled more employees than
were necessary to process the expected amount of film.
Objectivetask complexity was operationalized as the amount of informationthat
the participant needed to integrate in order to create an optimal work schedule.
Participants in the Complex condition needed to consider two types of information
that participants in the Simple condition did not. First, in the Simple condition,
participants were told to assume that the store expects 16 rolls of film to come in
to the store each hour, no matter what day, or what time of day it is. However, in
the Complex condition, participants were given a chart that showed how much film
is expected during different days and times during the day. The expected amount
of film varied from 9 to 24 rolls (but averaged 16 rolls). Therefore, in the Complex
condition, the store expected some times of the week to be slow and some times to
be busy. To maximize estimated profit, the participant needed to use this information to decide how many employees were needed to work.

employees asked not to be scheduled. Participants in this condition were penalized

Procedure
Participants worked individually, either in a room by themselves or in a room of 2
to 8 participants (mean group size = 2.69 participants); no communication among
participants was allowed. Each group (or individual) was randomly assigned to
either the Simple or Complex condition by coin toss. A brief overview of the
experimental procedure and participants' goal for the task was read. Any questions
participants had were answered, and then they read and signed informed consent
forms.
To further orient the participants on the task to be performed, an example of
scheduling work shifts for one day was presented on an overhead projector. This
example showed how to take into account certain rules while scheduling, and how
to use the scheduling sheets that would be provided to write down the shifts
accurately. The rules presented were that (a) each employee could work only one
shift per day, (b) work shifts had to be at least 3 hr long, and (c) at least two
employees had to be scheduled to work for every open hour of the store. It was
pointed out that if all rules were being followed, each participant could structure
the week's work shifts any way they wished. The example used the same seven
employee names as the actual scheduling task.
When all participants understood the example, packets were handed out that
included (a) a scheduling practice sheet, (b) the instructions for the actual scheduling task, and (c) scheduling sheets for the entire week. Participants studied the
instructions at the top of the page while they were read aloud by the experimenter.
The practice task involved scheduling work shifts for 2 days. The same three rules
presented in the previous example were also presented in the practice exercise. In
addition, participants were told that each employee could process eight rolls of film
per hour, and that the store expected to get 16 rolls of film in each hour during
Monday and Tuesday. Participants were then instructed to create a schedule using
the two scheduling sheets (one for each day), with the goal of maximizing estimated
profit. As with the example presented on the overhead projector, the employee
names on the practice exercise were consistent with those used in the actual task.
Participants were given as much time as they needed to complete the practice
exercise. If participants were in a group, they were instructed to wait until everyone
had finished the practice exercise before turning to the task instruction page.

DISCUSSION
The central thesis of this research, that objective and subjectivetask complexity are
actually different concepts that both predict task performance uniquely, was supported. Not only was the relation between objective and subjectivetask complexity
relatively modest (r = .34), the stepwiseregression analysis performed on subjective
task complexity revealed that perceptions of complexity were determined not only
by objective task complexity, but by cognitive ability and task motivation as well.
Therefore, neither ignoring perceptions of task complexity, nor using objective and
subjective task complexity interchangeably, is advised. Future complexity research
and theory need to include both the characteristics of the task and the perceptions
of the individual as separate sources of variance. Furthermore,as Ryan et al. (1992)
suggested, the mental workload literature can shed light on the two different
complexity constructs and their relations.
It was also found that the effects of objective task complexity and cognitive
ability on task performance were partially mediated by subjective task complexity.
That is, high objective task complexity and low cognitive ability led to perceptions
that the task was complex rather than simple, which in turn decreased task
performance.It may be that the negative relation between perceptions of complexity
and task performance is itself mediated by self-efficacy, such that perceptions of
complexity decrease one's confidence in successful completion of the task; this
decrease in self-efficacy has already been linked with lower performance (e.g., Gist
& Mitchell, 1992).
In addition to the indirect effects found through subjective task complexity, both
objective task complexity and cognitive ability also had direct effects on performance; these variables appear to place limits on the range of possible task performance. For example, performance is likely to be low if the task is extremely complex
(objectively),or if the individual performing the task has very low cognitive ability,
regardless of perceptions of complexity or simplicity.Within those limits, however,
perceptions of the task will influence task outcomes.
As predicted, perceptions of task complexity increased task motivation to
perform the task well, and this relation was moderated by cognitive ability. High
subjective task complexity was more motivating for those participants with high

Study Limitations
This study should be seen as an initial step in a larger program of research aimed
at understanding a set of task complexity constructs that are meaningful in both
organizational and cognitive contexts. Thus, there are several limitations of this

motivation and subjective task complexity prior to task completion.One possibility
is for participants to preview the task materials and instructions, and then report
